One clear rule for every teacher

When a student has an unexcused absence on an assessment day, the teacher should not have to decide the penalty. This tool gives one fixed deduction for each case. Every teacher gets the same answer for the same situation.

Step 4: A worked example

An AP student misses a test with an unexcused absence.
  1. The base for a test is 30%.
  2. AP leans 60% toward accountability, so the multiplier is 1.2.
  3. 30% × 1.2 = a 36% deduction.
  4. The test is worth 100 points, so 36 points come off.
  5. The student earns 85 on the make-up. 85 − 36 = 49 points recorded.

Every student loses the same number of points for the same absence, whatever they score. A recorded score never drops below 0.

If the student does not attend the teacher-arranged make-up session, the score is an automatic 0.

Why these numbers hold up

Schools handle missed assessments in very different ways. Some take off 10%. Some give a zero with no make-up. This policy sits in the middle. Its lowest deduction is 8% and its highest is 36%.

Where this policy sits

What other schools do

  • Broward County, Florida: high school students can make up work after an unexcused absence. Teachers may record a 10% reduction on tests and major projects. [1]
  • Fairfax County, Virginia: late summative work is accepted with a penalty of no more than 10% if it comes in within two weeks. [2]
  • Escambia County, Florida: teachers may accept make-up work and tests from unexcused absences for partial credit, with no more than one letter grade taken off. [3]
  • District 230, Illinois: every teacher scores the make-up as normal, then records it at 75% of the earned score. That is a 25% deduction. [4]
  • A Nevada high school department: make-up work turned in after two weeks earns only 60% credit. [5]
  • Schools with no make-up: one private school gives a zero on any quiz or test missed during an unexcused absence. [6] One international school does not allow a retake at all. [7]

What the research says

  • A clear penalty changes behavior. In a high school chemistry study, a 20% late penalty cut the amount of late work by more than half. [8]
  • Zeros distort the grade. Grading researchers warn that a zero for missing work, once averaged in, no longer shows what the student knows. It is also hard to recover from. [9] This is why a student who completes the make-up gets a fixed deduction instead of a zero.
  • Teachers need an alternative to the zero. One assessment expert argues that if schools ban zeros, they should give teachers another consequence to use. [10] A fixed, published deduction is that alternative.
  • Removing consequences carries risk. A 2024 Fordham Institute review found that lenient grading policies can lower expectations and lead to less learning. [11] In a 2025 national survey with RAND, most of the 967 teachers asked saw policies like no late penalties as harmful to student engagement. [12]

The policy keeps a real cost for skipping. It also keeps the student's actual work visible in the grade.

Common questions

Why do AP classes have a higher deduction than electives?
AP courses prepare students for an outside exam and expect more independence, so a missed assessment carries a higher cost.
What if the AU is later changed to excused?
The deduction is removed, and any change to the AU designation needs principal approval.
What does this policy cover?
It applies only to assessments missed because of an AU, never to excused absences or work missed for any other reason.
What about group presentations and projects?
Each group member is graded independently, so the deduction applies only to the student with the AU.
What if a student skips only the class with the assessment?
Skipping a single period on an assessment day may be truancy, and how to handle it is to be discussed with the principal.

For the principal to decide

  • Repeat AUs. Right now the fifth AU costs the same as the first. Should repeat AUs carry a bigger deduction or a referral?
  • Single-period truancy. Some students skip only the class with the assessment. Should that carry a harsher consequence than missing a full day?

How it saves teacher time

  • No penalty decisions case by case.
  • No corrections or re-dos.
  • One published rule that students and parents can see.
  • A clear cost for skipping should mean fewer make-ups over time.
